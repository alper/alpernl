# Drafts

Here’s an interesting idea: Developers should go through a proficiency test before they are allowed to use LLMs to generate code. An AI driver’s license of sorts.

This is a similar line of reasoning how software shops would ask an amount of apprenticeship from junior engineers before they would get push access or review credentials on a repository. Similarly, before we allow somebody to run loose with the code generating potential of a LLM, we should make sure they know how to wield it safely.

Using a LLM for learning and doing research of course be unlimited. Just that they can't spend all their time trying to one shot their tasks. Working that way is too tempting for somebody who doesn't know better.

Both sides would benefit from this arrangement.

## Company

It's obvious that LLMs function as an amplifier. Those who are skilled, can wield them to become even better and much more effective. Those who aren't, will use the tool to pretend they're better than they are and paper over their gaps in knowledge and effective delivery.

The company will always demand more of its people and that almost ensures that the technology will be abused to meet and surpass commitments no matter what the externalities are.

To be able to utilize the productivity increases of LLMs without allowing some of your engineers to destroy the coherence of the codebase, some gating will go a long way. Together with the gate, there should be a clear path how to go through it. That path would go through either a test of some sorts or you would clear the gate when you hit a predefined career level.

## Engineer

For the engineer the equation is even more clear cut. Prompting a LLM and doomscrolling while you wait for the output is a direct path to deskill yourself.

If you’re not continuously adding value in intent, knowledge and nous to the LLM loop, you are on your way out of the industry. You need to be on top of the code and everything the LLM does and you need to know exactly what is happening, what it's doing, why and how in many cases the suggested solution is deficient.

For a junior this is all but impossible. Just telling the LLM to fix lint until your change passes CI and then hoping somebody else will approve your code to merge, is a losing proposition.

If it's purely the output you're after, the best and most effective way to get there would be simply to learn how to touch type. For many people a LLM isn't much more than code complete anyway.

But the junior engineer needs to invest to get to the point where they can use the LLM effectively and that will only happen if they invest in their craft diligently and reflectively. The research and learning modes of the LLM are perfect for this. If there's no expectation that you will one-shot thousands of lines of code every day, you will get the space to invest in getting better.

A driver’s license for LLMs is of course not a perfect solution but I think it would remove a lot of the sharp edges af the usage we are seeing right now. At the very least it will make it clear that not all LLM usage is equal.

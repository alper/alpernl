As a software engineering manager, I can see around me that certain performance gaps are impossible to address on the shorter timelines required by the business. This issue has its roots in a macro-economical phenomenon called the discount rate and it affects professionals at all levels. 

The discount rate has gone up sharply. This affects investments at the macroscale in the environment and infrastructure as is exlpained in [this NYRB review](https://archive.is/S5LR6#selection-1443.0-1443.64) about the book “Discounting the Future: The Ascendancy of a Political Technology”.

In short, the higher the discount rate, the more future returns on investment are discounted. That means they are seen as either low in value or likelihood. A highly volatile geopolitical environment like the one we're in now casts doubt on future returns. Increasing interests rates and asset returns after a long period of 0% let money be deployed elsewhere readily for similar or better returns.

In software engineering we're noticing this effect in timelines shrinking drastically. Returns have to come faster, investment in the too distant future is not worth it.

That plays out in the architectural decisions companies make but stack and programming language changes come with their deep path dependence. The talent outlay of your organization is in comparison a lot more malleable and fungible.

That means companies will hire for readily available skills and try to rule out gaps as much as possible for their desired roles. Developing people on the job becomes less and less attractive.

This has created a nearly impossible market for junior software engineers who normally would expect to join an established company and get experience on the job including learning many skills that you will not pick up in any course of education. Those kind of positions have become hard to justify on the side of the business.

The trouble that people have finding an entry level (junior) job in this market has already been widely noticed. I would argue the same phenomenon applies to engineers at all levels but that it manifests in different ways.

Just as it has become increasingly more difficult as a business to advocate for hiring and developing entry level talent, that same calculus also applies to mid to senior level software engineers.

There are a set of fundamental skills where gaps are hard to close and with the current short term outlook are not worth developing from the perspective of the business.

Off the top of my head, I would list these as:

- Lack of metacognitive skills and sharpness
- Lack of fluency in English (or other dominant language)
- Inability to argue clearly in spoken and written word
- Lack of software engineering fundamentals

Not all of these skills will be necessary for every job, but for a position where they are, a gap on these can be fatal.

There are lots of ICs who are entering the industry based on a different economic climate and assumptions who may find themselves having a gap on one of these which makes them hard to employ.

They're asking themselves why finding a job is taking so long and feels so hopeless. This is also in part why the hiring process in these areas is so drawn out, onerous and arbitrary. Companies and hiring managers are trying to make sure they rule out any such gaps.

As a manager you may not even have the skill set to help somebody else close one of these gaps. These are skills that are wide and deep and difficult to learn, let alone to teach.

Even if you feel up to that challenge, this kind of development used to take on the order of 1-2 years to bear fruit. That is simply too long for the timelines we are dealing with right now. If you take a project like that on, you will need to see clear commitment and significant uplift within the first 1-2 months to be able to say that this is going somewhere.
